\begin{abstract}
Low-precision training is attractive for large-language-model post-training, but reinforcement-learning-based post-training introduces a distinct failure mode: the policy must generate its own training data. We find that replacing the linear layers of a BF16 policy with an NVFP4 simulation can corrupt early rollouts through repetition, mixed-language fragments, and prematurely terminated responses. These failures reduce the reliability of group-relative reward signals and prevent stable policy optimization. We present a post-training pipeline that first adapts the quantized policy with Mean self-distillation and then applies Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) using Mean NVFP4. The implementation includes rollout-quality and distribution diagnostics that make generation failures measurable during training, while the resulting low-precision policy is validated with actual NVFP4 inference. Preliminary controlled runs show substantially lower rule-based bad-rollout rates and higher rewards with Mean NVFP4 after Mean self-distillation than with NVFP4 GRPO.
% The final version will report matched comparisons with BF16, NVFP4, Hadamard NVFP4, and Mean NVFP4 across Qwen2.5 0.5B, Qwen2.5-Math 1.5B, and Qwen2.5-Math 7B models.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Post-training aligns a pretrained language model with task objectives through supervised, preference, or reinforcement-learning signals. For reasoning models, reinforcement-learning (RL) post-training is particularly effective because it improves a policy using rewards assigned to its own sampled solutions. Modern policy-gradient objectives such as PPO and its group-relative variant GRPO make this optimization practical for large language models \citep{schulman2017ppo,shao2024deepseekmath}. Yet this capability is expensive: the policy must repeatedly store activations, generate multiple long completions per prompt, and evaluate their rewards. FP4 arithmetic offers an attractive route to reducing the memory and compute cost of this process, and recent FP4 systems have demonstrated that carefully designed quantization can support large-scale training and efficient inference \citep{nvidia2025nvfp4,huang2025qerl}.

RL post-training nevertheless poses a challenge that is absent from fixed-batch training and deployment-time quantization. The current policy is also a data generator: it produces candidate completions, which are scored and used to update that same policy. This closed loop amplifies small perturbations in the quantized forward path. A quantization error that only modestly changes logits can change a sampled completion, alter group-relative rewards, and shift the next policy update. The relevant question is therefore not only whether an FP4 model attains low loss on teacher-forced tokens, but whether it can generate the diverse, interpretable rollouts required to construct a useful RL learning signal.

We study the following question: \emph{how can an FP4 policy be made reliable enough to generate the rollouts required for RL post-training?} Our starting observation is empirical and operational. Enabling the NVFP4 path for a BF16 policy before GRPO produces nonsensical repetitions, mixed-language fragments, and very short outputs early in training. The problem is not explained by visible numerical overflow: the diagnostic runs report no NaN or Inf events and stable activation-magnitude summaries. Instead, degraded decoding yields low-information groups of responses, weakening the relative-reward signal on which GRPO relies.

Our solution separates low-precision adaptation from reward optimization. We call the first-stage method Mean self-distillation. Before GRPO, a BF16 teacher samples responses and supplies response-token logits to an FP4 student. The student is optimized with a masked distillation objective on the same responses that define the teacher's decoding distribution. The adapted student then enters GRPO with the same low-precision forward path. To preserve the benefit of Mean NVFP4 during causal generation, we maintain a token-counted activation mean after prompt prefill and update it at each decode step. This two-stage design targets the source of the instability---the rollout distribution---before policy optimization can amplify it.

This work makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a rollout-centric diagnosis of NVFP4 GRPO failure, supported by quantitative text-quality and training-signal diagnostics;
    \item Mean self-distillation, which adapts an FP4 policy to a BF16 teacher before reward optimization;
    \item a Mean NVFP4 implementation for both training and autoregressive decode; and
    \item a matched evaluation protocol spanning mathematical reasoning and general reasoning benchmarks.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{FP4 Quantized Training}

Training with FP4 is challenging because blockwise scales must represent both rare high-magnitude values and the much denser bulk of a tensor. Metis attributes this difficulty to spectral anisotropy and uses structured decomposition to quantize narrower sub-distributions \citep{cao2025metis}. Averis identifies a coherent activation mean as an important source of outliers and quantizes a centered residual \citep{cao2026averis}. Other FP4-training systems combine mixed-precision optimization, differentiable quantization, and outlier compensation to stabilize large-scale pretraining \citep{wang2025optimizing,chmiel2025fp4}. NVIDIA's Hadamard NVFP4 pretraining recipe combines random Hadamard transforms, two-dimensional quantization, stochastic rounding, and selective high-precision layers \citep{nvidia2025nvfp4}. In the post-training setting, QeRL combines NVFP4 weight quantization with LoRA and adaptive quantization noise to reduce rollout cost while optimizing reasoning policies \citep{huang2025qerl}.

\subsection{Post-Training Quantization for LLM Inference}

Post-training quantization (PTQ) converts a trained model using calibration data, without replaying its original optimization procedure. Early LLM PTQ work identified activation outliers as a central obstacle \citep{dettmers2022llmint8}; SmoothQuant shifts the activation-quantization burden into weights through an equivalent transformation \citep{xiao2023smoothquant}. Data-aware weight-only methods use approximate second-order information or activation-aware scaling to preserve important directions \citep{frantar2023gptq,lin2024awq}. Transformation-based methods reduce activation and weight outliers before quantization: QuaRot applies invariant rotations to hidden states, feed-forward activations, attention components, and KV caches \citep{ashkboos2024quarot}, whereas FlatQuant learns affine transformations that flatten layerwise weight and activation distributions \citep{sun2024flatquant}. OmniQuant instead learns clipping and equivalent transformations during calibration \citep{shao2024omniquant}. LLM-FP4 studies floating-point PTQ through format and clipping-range search together with per-channel activation scaling \citep{liu2023llmfp4}; QRazor instead retains salient bits from an integer representation to quantize weights, activations, and KV caches without additional training \citep{lee2025qrazor}. These methods target a fixed, deployment-time quantizer and inference accuracy. They do not address the coupled rollout-and-update process in which an RL policy generates the trajectories used for its own optimization.

\subsection{Low-Precision Adaptation and Distillation for Post-Training}

Fine-tuning with a simulated low-precision forward path can adapt a model to quantization error. Classical learned-quantization methods optimize quantizer parameters directly against task loss \citep{esser2020lsq}, while knowledge distillation provides the standard teacher--student distribution-matching objective \citep{hinton2015distilling}. For LLMs, prior work has combined self-distillation with sub-4-bit low-precision adaptation \citep{du2024bitdistiller}. This line of work establishes teacher--student distribution matching as a practical way to recover low-precision accuracy for post-trained models.

\paragraph{Motivation.} Taken together, existing FP4 training methods establish mechanisms for controlling quantization error during pretraining, and PTQ methods optimize a fixed model for inference. Low-precision adaptation can recover accuracy after a post-training pipeline, but it does not directly address the transition from a BF16 policy to an NVFP4 policy that must immediately generate on-policy trajectories. RL post-training instead couples quantization, decoding, reward construction, and policy updates in a single feedback loop. This setting motivates a rollout-centric NVFP4 pipeline: first apply Mean self-distillation to adapt the low-precision computation path to a stable teacher distribution, then optimize the adapted policy with RL while preserving a causal quantization state during decoding.

\section{Method}

\subsection{FP4 Post-Training with GRPO}

For a prompt $x$, GRPO samples a group of completions $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}$ from the current policy $\pi_\theta$. A task reward $r(x,y_i)$ is converted into a group-relative advantage, and the policy is updated with a clipped objective. Consequently, a useful optimization step presupposes that the sampled group contains interpretable responses with reward variation. If quantized decoding produces repeated or malformed text, relative comparisons can become noisy even when the reward function itself is correctly implemented.

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} provides an overview of the proposed two-stage pipeline before the component details below. It first adapts an FP4 student to a BF16 teacher through Mean self-distillation, then uses the adapted policy for GRPO with Mean NVFP4.

\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Figures/fp4_post_training_pipeline.pdf}

In Stage~I, the ice-blue teacher robot and snowflake denote frozen BF16 parameters, whereas the warm FP4 student robot is updated by masked token-level distillation. Stage~II uses the adapted Mean NVFP4 policy to generate GRPO rollouts and updates it using group-relative rewards. The grid blocks visualize the fixed weight decomposition $U_r\Sigma_rV_r^\top+R_W$ and the centered-residual decomposition used by Mean NVFP4 for activations and output gradients; only the residual matrices use FP4 quantization, and a causal activation-mean cache supplies the activation mean during autoregressive decoding.}
    \label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure*}

More concretely, let $\widehat A_i$ denote the reward advantage normalized within a group of $G$ completions, and let $\rho_i(\theta)$ be the ratio between the current and rollout-policy probabilities of $y_i$. The policy term takes the standard clipped form
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\theta)
&= -\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min\!\bigl(\rho_i(\theta)\widehat A_i, \\[-0.2ex]
&\hspace{2.2cm}\operatorname{clip}(\rho_i(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\widehat A_i\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Thus, the relevant failure is not merely a larger quantization error on a fixed batch: malformed samples can reduce both the semantic validity of $r(x,y_i)$ and the variation required to form useful within-group advantages.

The teacher and the BF16 reference policy use BF16 linear layers. For the FP4 policy, trainable master parameters remain in higher precision, while each selected linear projection is evaluated with FP4 operands in the forward and backward matrix multiplications. We apply a fixed weight-decomposition path and FP4 quantization to weights, activations, and gradients; the activation and gradient quantization paths define the experimental condition.

\subsection{Spectral Weight Factorization}

We factor each selected weight matrix before post-training. Let $W\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ and let its truncated rank-$r$ singular-value decomposition be
\begin{equation}
    W = U_r\Sigma_r V_r^{\top}+R_W,
    \qquad
    R_W \triangleq W-U_r\Sigma_r V_r^{\top},
    \label{eq:weight-svd}
\end{equation}
where $U_r\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r}$, $\Sigma_r\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}$, and $V_r\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}\times r}$. This decomposition follows the spectral motivation of Metis: a small number of large singular components can dominate the dynamic range of an otherwise quantization-friendly residual \citep{cao2025metis}. In our implementation, $r=64$ for the proposed condition. The low-rank right factor and the residual are evaluated through FP4 linear operators, whereas the left factor and singular values form the reconstruction path. Denoting quantize--dequantize in FP4 by $Q_{\mathrm{FP4}}$, the effective weight is
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde W
    = U_r\Sigma_r Q_{\mathrm{FP4}}(V_r^{\top})
      + Q_{\mathrm{FP4}}(R_W).
    \label{eq:weight-reconstruct}
\end{equation}
For a row-major activation matrix $X$, the corresponding forward GeMM is therefore $Y=\widehat X\widetilde W^{\top}+b$. Equivalently, the implementation first applies the quantized $V_r^{\top}$ projection, scales the rank-$r$ features by $\Sigma_r$, projects them with $U_r$, and adds the quantized residual branch. This form avoids forcing the dominant spectral component and the low-energy residual to share a single FP4 scale.

\subsection{Mean NVFP4}

Mean NVFP4 centers activations and output gradients around their feature-wise means before quantizing the residuals. Let $X\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ denote a flattened activation matrix, where $L$ merges batch and sequence positions. Following the mean-bias observation of Averis \citep{cao2026averis}, we isolate the feature-wise mean
\begin{equation}
    \mu_X = \frac{1}{L}\mathbf{1}_L^{\top}X,
    \qquad
    X_R = X-\mathbf{1}_L\mu_X,
    \label{eq:activation-split}
\end{equation}
and apply FP4 only to the centered residual:
\begin{equation}
    \widehat X
    = \mathbf{1}_L\mu_X+Q_{\mathrm{FP4}}(X_R).
    \label{eq:activation-qdq}
\end{equation}
Thus, Mean NVFP4 restores the shared offset in higher precision after quantizing the centered residual. Centering allocates the finite FP4 codebook to token-to-token variation rather than to a coherent rank-one offset. Although the implementation retains the generic Metis low-rank configuration interface, Mean NVFP4 selects this centered-residual path for activations rather than computing an activation SVD at every step.

The backward output gradient is treated analogously. For $D=\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial Y\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d_{\mathrm{out}}}$, define
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mu_D&=\frac{1}{L}\mathbf{1}_L^{\top}D,
        &D_R&=D-\mathbf{1}_L\mu_D,\\[-0.2ex]
        \widehat D&=\mathbf{1}_L\mu_D+Q_{\mathrm{FP4}}(D_R).
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:gradient-qdq}
\end{equation}
The low-precision linear backward path then uses
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\nabla_W\mathcal{L}}=\widehat D^{\top}\widehat X,
    \qquad
    \widehat{\nabla_X\mathcal{L}}=\widehat D\widetilde W.
    \label{eq:quantized-backward}
\end{equation}
Expanding the reconstructed low-precision weight-gradient GeMM yields four terms:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
    \widehat{\nabla_W\mathcal{L}}
    ={}& Q_{\mathrm{FP4}}(D_R)^{\top}Q_{\mathrm{FP4}}(X_R)
    + Q_{\mathrm{FP4}}(D_R)^{\top}\mathbf{1}_L\mu_X \\[-0.2ex]
    &+ \mu_D^{\top}\mathbf{1}_L^{\top}Q_{\mathrm{FP4}}(X_R)
    + L\mu_D^{\top}\mu_X.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:mean-gradient-identity}
\end{equation}
Equation~\ref{eq:mean-gradient-identity} is the form used by the Mean NVFP4 implementation: it quantizes the residual streams, restores the mean components, and evaluates the resulting product without cancelling any term. Quantize--dequantize changes the residuals, and the causal activation mean and token masks need not match the gradient's reduction domain. The computation therefore retains the residual--residual interaction, both cross interactions between means and residuals, and the rank-one mean--mean interaction.

During prompt prefill, the method computes $\mu_X$ over all available prompt positions and stores both the mean and the number of represented token positions. At autoregressive decode step $t$, let $\mu_t$ be the mean of the newly observed positions and $n_t$ their count. The cache is updated as
\begin{equation}
    \mu_{\mathrm{cache}} \leftarrow
    \frac{n_{\mathrm{cache}}\mu_{\mathrm{cache}}+n_t\mu_t}
    {n_{\mathrm{cache}}+n_t}.
    \label{eq:causal-mean-cache}
\end{equation}
At decode time, Eq.~\ref{eq:activation-qdq} uses $\mu_{\mathrm{cache}}$ in place of $\mu_X$. This construction does not access future generated tokens and therefore matches the information available to an autoregressive decoder. A full-sequence training forward and a prefix-only decode forward can nevertheless use different means; we discuss this inherent train--decode mismatch in the conclusion.

\subsection{Mean Self-Distillation Before GRPO}

The proposed pipeline has two stages. First, a frozen BF16 teacher samples a response $y$ for each prompt $x$. The teacher and FP4 student then perform full forwards on the same concatenated sequence $(x,y)$. Let $z_{M,b,t,v}$ denote the next-token logit of model $M\in\{T,S\}$ for vocabulary item $v$ at response position $t$ in minibatch example $b$. We select the teacher's top-$k$ set and renormalize both models over that same set:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{K}_{b,t}
&=\operatorname{TopK}_k\!\left(z_{T,b,t,\cdot}\right),\\
\widetilde p_{M,b,t}(v)
&=\frac{\exp(z_{M,b,t,v}/T)}
{\sum_{u\in\mathcal{K}_{b,t}}\exp(z_{M,b,t,u}/T)},
\quad v\in\mathcal{K}_{b,t}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Let $m_{b,t}\in\{0,1\}$ indicate a valid response token and let $N_{\mathrm{valid}}=\sum_{b,t}m_{b,t}$. The implemented objective is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KD}}
=\frac{T^2}{\max(N_{\mathrm{valid}},1)}
\sum_{b,t}m_{b,t}
\sum_{v\in\mathcal{K}_{b,t}}
\widetilde p_{T,b,t}(v)
\log\frac{\widetilde p_{T,b,t}(v)}
{\widetilde p_{S,b,t}(v)}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:topk-distillation}
\end{equation}
By default, $k=100$. We retain only the teacher's top-$k$ logits and indices and gather the corresponding student logits. Both distributions are renormalized over the teacher-selected subset, and the loss is averaged over all valid response tokens in the minibatch. Mean self-distillation thereby adapts the low-precision computation path to the teacher's dominant response distribution before the policy is used to explore and optimize rewards. In the second stage, the resulting FP4 student is trained with GRPO on DeepMath-103K examples using a mathematical-equivalence reward.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Mean Self-Distillation Followed by FP4 GRPO}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE Initialize BF16 teacher $\pi_T$ and FP4 student $\pi_S$ from the same base checkpoint.
\FOR{each Mean self-distillation minibatch of prompts $x$}
    \STATE Sample teacher responses $y\sim\pi_T(\cdot\mid x)$.
    \STATE Update $\pi_S$ with the masked, top-$k$-normalized distillation loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KD}}$.
\ENDFOR
\FOR{each GRPO minibatch of prompts $x$}
    \STATE Sample quantized rollouts $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}$ from $\pi_S$ using the causal activation-mean cache of Mean NVFP4.
    \STATE Compute task rewards and group-relative advantages.
    \STATE Update $\pi_S$ with the GRPO objective.
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Models, Data, and Baselines}

The study post-trains Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct \citep{yang2024qwen25}, Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Base, and Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Base \citep{yang2024qwen25math} on DeepMath-103K \citep{he2025deepmath}, a collection of 103,022 challenging mathematical problems curated for RL and supervised fine-tuning. The dataset provides a verifiable final answer for each problem, enabling rule-based rewards, and is constructed through benchmark decontamination and difficulty filtering. We use its problem prompts and final answers for GRPO; Mean self-distillation uses teacher-generated responses on the same prompt distribution.

The final matched comparison contains four conditions: (i) the pretrained checkpoint without post-training, (ii) BF16 GRPO, (iii) Hadamard NVFP4 GRPO, and (iv) Mean NVFP4 GRPO after Mean self-distillation.

We use EvalScope to evaluate every reported checkpoint on AIME 2024, AIME 2025, AMC, MATH-500 \citep{lightman2023letsverify}, ARC \citep{clark2018arc}, and GPQA-Diamond \citep{rein2023gpqa}. Each prompt receives one greedy completion with \texttt{do\_sample=false},  and a maximum of 2048 newly generated tokens. For Mean NVFP4 checkpoints trained with Mean self-distillation, evaluation uses actual NVFP4 inference rather than only the simulated low-precision path used during training. We report the task accuracy produced by the corresponding EvalScope report.

\subsection{Rollout Diagnostics}

Scalar benchmark accuracy is insufficient to explain why a post-training run succeeds or fails. The system therefore records per-completion and per-step text-quality statistics: empty and short outputs, non-printable-character ratio, repeated $n$-grams, consecutive repeated tokens, lexical diversity, punctuation and symbol ratios, and multi-language switching. It aggregates a rule-based bad-rollout rate, reward statistics, and separate trends for responses classified as good and bad. Complementary diagnostics record activations and rollout-related probability summaries. The bad-rollout label is multi-label: a completion can be assigned more than one failure type.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Mean Self-Distillation Stabilizes FP4 Rollouts Before GRPO}
\label{sec:mean-self-distillation-analysis}

We first ask whether a BF16 policy can be used under the NVFP4 execution path to generate the trajectories required by GRPO. Figure~\ref{fig:nvfp4-bad-types} decomposes the NVFP4 failures behind the aggregate bad-rollout rate. Low lexical diversity and explicit token loops account for a large fraction of flagged completions, with short outputs and mixed-language generations providing additional evidence of decoding degradation. Because the categories are assigned independently, their proportions need not sum to one. This decomposition is important: it distinguishes a high bad-rollout rate caused by visible generation failures from a mere change in task difficulty or reward calibration.

\paragraph{Training-Signal Comparison.} Table~\ref{tab:reward} reports the first and final logged GRPO rewards for each model and precision condition. These endpoints expose whether the policy obtains a useful learning signal under the FP4 rollout path.

NVFP4 produces the weakest final reward at every scale. Its failure is most pronounced at 0.5B and 1.5B: reward rises only from 0.0200 to 0.0398 for the former and falls from 0.0820 to 0.0191 for the latter. Mean NVFP4 after Mean self-distillation changes this behavior. It raises the 0.5B reward to 0.2316 and the 1.5B reward to 0.4023, approaching the corresponding BF16 values of 0.3066 and 0.4378. At 7B, both NVFP4 variants improve during GRPO, but Mean NVFP4 finishes higher than NVFP4 (0.3379 versus 0.2949), while BF16 remains highest at 0.4448. The improvement is therefore not simply a consequence of a favorable starting reward: the two NVFP4 conditions begin at comparable levels for the 0.5B and 7B models, whereas their final rewards separate after training. Combined with the rollout diagnostics, this pattern supports Mean self-distillation as an effective preparation step for obtaining a usable Mean NVFP4 GRPO learning signal.

\subsection{Empirical Takeaway}

The results form a consistent picture of FP4 post-training. NVFP4 degrades rollout quality and yields the weakest final reward across all three model scales, demonstrating that low-precision execution can disrupt the on-policy data on which GRPO depends. Mean NVFP4, prepared through Mean self-distillation, addresses this failure before reward optimization: it substantially improves the NVFP4 reward trajectory and enables strong downstream reasoning performance for the math-specialized 1.5B and 7B models. The remaining gap on selected tasks, most clearly ARC for the 7B model and MATH-500 for the 0.5B instruction model, identifies robustness across model families as the central challenge for FP4 post-training.

\section{Conclusion}

FP4 post-training cannot be evaluated solely as a low-precision optimization problem because an RL policy generates the trajectories from which it learns. We presented a two-stage FP4 pipeline that first adapts a low-precision student to a BF16 teacher through Mean self-distillation and then applies GRPO with Mean NVFP4. This ordering targets the rollout distribution before reward optimization closes the feedback loop.

Across three model scales, NVFP4 produces degraded rollout quality and the weakest final reward. Mean NVFP4 after Mean self-distillation substantially improves the NVFP4 learning signal and preserves strong downstream reasoning performance for the 1.5B and 7B math-specialized models. These findings establish rollout quality as a central criterion for FP4 RL post-training: a low-precision policy must generate usable candidate groups, not merely execute low-precision matrix multiplications on a fixed batch.

The remaining challenges clarify the scope of the approach. The causal activation-mean cache used by Mean NVFP4 can differ from a full-sequence training forward, and the rule-based rollout classifier captures visible degeneration rather than every form of semantic failure. Moreover, the ARC and 0.5B results show that stable FP4 optimization does not guarantee uniform accuracy across all model families and tasks. Reporting the complete quantization recipe, decoding configuration, hardware and software environment, and seed-level variation is therefore essential for reproducible FP4 post-training research.
